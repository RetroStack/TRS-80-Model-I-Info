# The serial interface

Four ports, a UART, a baud-rate generator that runs the two directions independently, and no
interrupt anyone can use. Model I serial software polls, and it polls because of a decision
made on the Expansion Interface rather than on the serial card.

The card reading is [`schematics-model-1-rs232.md`](schematics-model-1-rs232.md); this document
is the subsystem, including the half of it that lives on the Expansion Interface.

## Where the ports come from

The card does not decode its own address space from scratch. **The Expansion Interface decodes
eight ports, `E8`–`EF`**, on sheet 4: `Z46B` (74LS20) tests `A7·A6·A5·A3`, `Z28D` inverts `A4`,
and `Z28C` combines them, so `/E8` asserts for `A7 A6 A5 = 111`, `A4 = 0`, `A3 = 1`. `A2`, `A1`
and `A0` pass through to the card on `J10`, along with `D0`–`D7`, `/IN`, `/OUT`, `/SYSRES` and
`/INT`.

**The card claims the lower four.** It inverts `/E8` and `A2` and NANDs them, which qualifies
the window down to `A2 = 0` — `E8`–`EB`. It simply refuses to answer when `A2` is high, so
`EC`–`EF` are decoded by the Expansion Interface and driven by nothing.

A 74LS155 then splits `A1`/`A0` into four read strobes and four write strobes, the read side
gated by `/IN` and the write side by `/OUT`.

## The four ports

| port | read | write |
|---|---|---|
| `E8` | modem status in | modem control out |
| `E9` | **nothing** | baud rate |
| `EA` | UART status | UART frame format |
| `EB` | received data | transmit data |

**On the rebuild, `E9` is write-only**: the read strobe exists on the decoder and goes
nowhere, so a read leaves the bus undriven. Period documentation for the original 26-1145 card
describes a "sense switches" read there, and the TERM program reads `E9` for them; the rebuild is
silent on what the original card had (see *What the card is* below).

### `E8` — modem status and control

A 74LS367 buffers five signals onto the bus when read, and they land on **`D7`, `D6`, `D5`, `D4`
and `D0`** — its sixth channel is unused, so `D3`, `D2` and `D1` have no buffer and float high.
The five come from the line receivers: `CTS`, `DSR`, `CD`, `RI` and the received-data line.

A 74LS174 latches **`D0`, `D1` and `D2`** on the write strobe, driving `DTR`, `RTS` and a
transmit gate — the last AND-ed with the UART's serial output so the line can be held in a break
condition. Its `/Mr` is tied high through a 4.7 kΩ resistor, so **`/SYSRES` does not clear it**:
a reset leaves the modem control lines exactly where they were.

### `E9` — the baud rate generator

`D0`–`D3` set the receive divisor and `D4`–`D7` the transmit divisor of a COM8116, so **the two
directions can run at different rates**. With the card's 5.0688 MHz crystal the sixteen divisors
give the standard rates from 50 to 19200 baud.

### `EA` — status in, format out

Reading returns the UART's status pins: parity error, framing error, overrun, data received,
transmit buffer empty and transmit register empty.

Writing sets the frame format — parity inhibit on `D3`, stop-bit select on `D4`, word length on
`D5` and `D6`, even-parity select on `D7` — latched by the UART's control-register load.

**An idle UART reports its transmitter empty.** A status register that reads zero says
"transmitter never empty", and every driver that polls for readiness before writing will hang on
it. Zero is not merely an incomplete answer here; it is the one value that deadlocks.

### `EB` — data

Reading takes the receive buffer, writing loads the transmit buffer and pulses the load line.

## Why Model I serial software polls

`/INT` reaches the card on `J10`, so a card in that slot **can** pull the interrupt line. Nothing
reports it. Sheet 1 of the Expansion Interface buffers exactly two sources onto the bus for a
read of `37E0` — the 40 Hz timer on `D7` and the floppy controller on `D6` — and there is no
third bit and no third buffer.

So an interrupt from the serial card is indistinguishable from one it did not cause: the handler
reads `37E0`, sees neither the timer nor the floppy, and has nothing to go on. **The card can
raise an interrupt that no software can attribute to it**, which is why every Model I serial
driver polls `EA` instead.

## What the card is, and what that costs

The only available reconstruction is a **modern rebuild** rather than a reconstruction of the
original 26-1145: the 1488/1489 line drivers are replaced by a single SP3243 and the TR1602 by
its pin-compatible HD6402. It is evidence about **its own** programming model, which the
emulator follows — not about what was inside a 1979 card. The port map above (the `E8` modem
latch on `D0`–`D2`, no `E9` read, the `EA` format bits) is the rebuild's; the original card's
service schematic and the period TERM program describe a reset on an `E8` write, sense switches
read at `E9` and the modem-control bits on `EA`, and nothing here settles which the original had.
Component-level statements describe the rebuild.

The line interface carries five receivers — `CTS`, `DSR`, `CD`, `RI`, `RXD` — and three
transmitters — `DTR`, `RTS`, `TXD`, plus signal ground. **There is no hardware handshake logic**:
the modem control lines are latched out and buffered in, and any flow control is the software's
business.

## What this settles

1. **The Expansion Interface decodes `E8`–`EF`; the card answers on `E8`–`EB`.** Eight decoded,
   four used, four driven by nothing.
2. **`E9` is write-only** — no sense-switch read exists.
3. **`E8` read drives only `D7`, `D6`, `D5`, `D4` and `D0`**; the other three float high.
4. **A reset does not clear the modem control latch.**
5. **The two directions can run at different baud rates**, from one generator.
6. **An idle UART must report its transmitter empty**, or a polling driver deadlocks.
7. **The serial card cannot report itself in the `37E0` buffers**, which is why Model I serial
   software polls.
